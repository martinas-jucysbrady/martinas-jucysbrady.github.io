Ulster — Ruins, Coastline, and the Aurora

Ulster holds more than most people who live in it realise. The landscape shifts across it — the lakelands of Fermanagh, the basalt coast of Antrim, the mountains of Mourne, the headlands of Donegal — and each corner has something that would draw visitors from anywhere else in the world if it were somewhere else in the world.

Crom Castle on the shores of Upper Lough Erne is one of the better estate grounds in Ireland — the lake visible between the old trees, the ruins of the earlier castle sitting above it, the whole place largely to yourself on most days. Castle Saunderson nearby in Co. Fermanagh is more dramatic in its ruin — the massive complex abandoned and returned to nature, the old church on the grounds still standing with a particular quality of light in the afternoon. Places like this are everywhere in Ireland and mostly unmarked on any tourist map.

Navan Fort in Co. Armagh has the quarry beside it — the water is that specific shade of blue that old quarries in limestone occasionally produce, the kind that makes you check whether it’s been artificially coloured. The Giants Causeway on the north Antrim coast is one of the genuine natural wonders of the island, the hexagonal basalt columns extending into the sea and the waves working against them — the sound and the spray, in any weather, make it a different experience from the photographs. Portstewart Strand along the same coast is one of the better beaches in the country.

The aurora, seen from home on one of the nights in 2024 when it was visible across Ireland and Britain — the kind of thing you stand in the garden and look at for a long time, unable to quite believe it is happening at your latitude, above your own house.

Galboly in Co. Antrim is one of the most beautiful places in Ireland and almost nobody knows it. A deserted clachan on a clifftop above the North Channel — the famine-era houses preserved, the thatched roofs reconstructed, the mountains behind the settlement and Scotland visible across the water on a clear day. Free to walk around, entirely uncommercialized. The Silent Valley Mountain Park in the Mournes frames the reservoir between the peaks in a way that makes the dam look like it was placed rather than built. Cairdhnu House near Belfast — a massive abandoned country house that you can walk through inside and out, the interior open to the elements, the grounds reverting slowly to woodland. The carginagh sunflower fields in August. Rossnowlagh Beach in Donegal, the waves long and rolling and the sand wide. Spelga Dam at sunset, the light coming across the reservoir and the hills above it.

The aurora from home — above your own house, at your own latitude, impossible to quite believe.

Leinster — Dublin and the East

Dublin is a city that takes a certain amount of familiarity to appreciate properly. The Spire on O’Connell Street, which most Dubliners have a complicated relationship with, is genuinely striking from certain angles in certain light. Temple Bar at Christmas, the pub lit up and decorated, is the tourist image of the city and one that earns its place. The Aviva in full voice for an Ireland rugby international is a different kind of experience — the stadium compact and loud in the way that smaller grounds tend to be, the crowd close to the pitch.

Carlingford is one of the better small towns in the country — the medieval ruins, the mountain above it, the lough on the other side, the whole thing compact and charming. Blackrock near Dundalk is the kind of local gem that most counties have and most visitors miss: a pretty village on a tidal estuary where the beach at low tide extends so far out that you can walk across to the sandbar in the middle of the channel. Edenvale Woods has a waterfall and a swimming spot that earns the drive. Curracloe Beach in Wexford is long, wide, and excellent.

The World Darts Championship in Dublin: the crowd at the Ally Pally of Dublin, the atmosphere matching the original.

Connacht — The West

The west of Ireland operates at a different pace and in a different light. Rosses Point in Co. Sligo, looking out over the Atlantic with Knocknarea above it, is the kind of view that makes you understand why people who leave the west keep talking about it. Streedagh Beach along the Sligo coast — the long walk along the strand, the dunes behind, the sea ahead — is the beach that most Sligo people would point you to over the more famous ones.

Gleniff Horseshoe is the drive worth making in Sligo — a loop road through a deep glacial valley ringed by limestone cliffs, the hillsides often in cloud, a waterfall at the old mill sites visible from the road. Remote, quiet, and the kind of landscape that makes you understand the word sublime in the original sense.

Scardan Waterfall in Co. Leitrim requires the drive into the back hills and the walk, and both reward. The viewpoint beside it looks out over a valley and lake system that has no visible human intrusion — the kind of landscape that Ireland holds more of than most people who live here realise. The Shannon by boat is the correct way to experience the river midlands — the pace of the water, the bankside vegetation, the locks.

The Shannon by boat — the correct pace for the river midlands.

“Ireland holds more beauty than most people who live here realise — and most of it is unmarked.”

Travelling far enough eventually produces a particular kind of appreciation for home — not the sentimental kind, but the specific, grounded kind that comes from having seen enough elsewhere to know what comparison looks like. Ireland holds extraordinary landscapes, extraordinary history, and extraordinary light, and most of it exists without fanfare or a ticket queue.

The aurora from your own garden. The cliffside village that nobody visits. The waterfall in the back hills. The beach that extends to the horizon in low tide. None of it requires a flight.

Galboly — Scotland on the horizon, the Atlantic below, home behind you.
