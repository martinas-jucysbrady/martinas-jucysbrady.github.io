Tokyo and beyond — the city that has everything

2024 – 2025 📍 Tokyo · Kanagawa · Chiba

Tokyo — Everything You Can Think Of

Tokyo is not a city that rewards a single visit. It is a city that rewards return visits, each one revealing a different layer of something that doesn’t have an obvious bottom. The districts operate at different registers and take time to understand individually — Shibuya is kinetic and vertical, Shinjuku is dense and multilayered, Asakusa is historical and open, Roppongi is polished and elevated, Shin-Okubo is Korea transplanted into a Tokyo neighbourhood and entirely itself.

The Shibuya crossing is the most famous pedestrian crossing in the world and is exactly as extraordinary in person as the footage suggests — the scale of it, the coordination of it, the sheer number of people moving in every direction at once and somehow not colliding. Shibuya Sky, the observation deck above it, gives the city from above in a way that makes the crossing below look like a circuit board.

Shinjuku Gyoen in cherry blossom season is the reason to time a visit carefully. The blossoms in the park are the brightest pink, the crowds are large but the park is large enough, and the combination of the flowers and the skyscrapers visible over the treeline is the Tokyo image. The 3D cat billboard outside the building on the east side of Shinjuku station is better in person than in video. The Godzilla head above the Toho building is exactly as good as it should be.

TeamLab Planets and TeamLab Borderless are two different experiences from the same organisation and both are worth doing separately. Planets is immersive in the physical sense — you walk through the exhibits, some of them ankle-deep in water, the reflections doubling and tripling the space. Borderless is more expansive, the boundaries between rooms dissolving, the art moving between spaces. Both reward going without expectations of what interactive digital art is supposed to feel like.

Asakusa is the counterpoint to all of it — the Senso-ji temple complex, ancient and active, with the skyscrapers of modern Tokyo framed above the old gate in one of the more striking juxtapositions the city offers. The Tokyo Skytree rises above the neighbourhood and from the top at night the city extends in every direction to the horizon, the grid of light below making the scale of what Tokyo actually is finally apparent.

The food deserves its own section. Omurice at the place with the thirty-minute queue is worth every minute — egg folded over rice in a way that the photographs do not explain and the eating does. Rice & Circus is exactly what the name doesn’t explain: a restaurant in Shinjuku serving unusual proteins alongside goofy cocktails infused with ingredients that range from unexpected to genuinely alarming. The swallow, the camel, the bear, the whale, the crocodile tempura — all of it surprisingly edible. The Nepalese food in Shin-Okubo, specifically the honey cheese naan, is essential. The pig café is what it sounds like and better than expected.

Shibuya crossing — every direction at once, somehow nobody colliding.

“Tokyo is not a city that rewards a single visit.”

Kanagawa — Islands, Buddhas, and Hakone

Kanagawa is where Tokyo breathes out. Enoshima is a small island connected to the mainland by a causeway, full of history, temples, sea caves, and the kind of scenery that makes a day trip from the city feel like a different world. The sunset from the island with Fuji silhouetted on the horizon is the image — the mountain just visible, the sky doing everything.

Kamakura is the historical anchor of the prefecture — the Great Buddha, one of the largest bronze statues in Japan, sitting in the open air at the end of a wooded approach, is as impressive in person as the scale of it promises. The shrine above the town gives views down over the rooftops and the sea beyond.

Hakone is the day trip that delivers most consistently. Owakudani — the volcanic valley with hot springs venting from the hillside — produces the onsen eggs with the black shells from the sulphur, the taste of which is influenced, inevitably, by the smell of the whole place. The floating torii gate at Hakone Shrine, standing in the lake with the mountains behind it, is mesmerising. Odawara Castle on the way back is a well-restored castle worth stopping for. The sushi in this region is excellent throughout — proximity to the sea making itself apparent.

Enoshima at sunset — Fuji on the horizon, just visible, the sky doing everything.

Chiba — Tokyo Disneyland

Tokyo Disneyland is a different experience from its American counterparts — cleaner, more theatrical, the attention to detail at a level that makes the original parks look slightly less committed. The Baymax curry is the food to order. The castle is surreal in the specific way that Disney castles are when they exist in the middle of a Japanese city, visible from the train as you approach.

“Tokyo is a city that rewards return visits, each one revealing a different layer of something that doesn’t have an obvious bottom.”

Kanto is anchored by Tokyo but defined by more than it. The city provides the base — inexhaustible, always different, endlessly layered — and the prefecture around it provides the counterpoint: the island at sunset with Fuji on the horizon, the Buddha in the forest, the volcanic valley, the floating shrine on the lake. Each weekend away from the city confirms that the region around it is as varied as the city itself.

Come back. Keep coming back. There is always more.

Kanto — and always, somewhere on the horizon, Fuji.
