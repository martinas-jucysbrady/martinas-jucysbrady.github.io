Kansai

Home for a year — Kobe, Kyoto, Osaka, and everything in between

2024 – 2025 📍 Hyogo · Kyoto · Shiga · Osaka · Nara · Wakayama

Kobe — Home

Kobe was home for twelve months, and the city earned that designation without much effort. It is compact enough to know well and varied enough to keep revealing itself — the harbour at night, the Chinatown lit by lanterns, the western-style buildings of Kitano on the hillside, the mountains rising directly behind the city in a way that makes it feel enclosed and protected. Mt. Maya above the city offers one of the best night views in Japan.

Harbourland concentrated most of the city’s events across the year — the Christmas markets, the Luminaire illuminations that commemorate the Great Hanshin Earthquake every December, the firework display over the water, a Christmas drone show that was better than expected. The combination of the harbour lights and the illuminated structures is genuinely beautiful on a winter evening.

Kobe Animal Kingdom is one of the better animal parks in Japan — the tiger enclosure close enough to be slightly alarming, the lemur enclosure walkthrough, the capybara petting area. The Nunobiki falls and herb gardens above the city are reached by ropeway and give views over the port and the bay. Venus Bridge at night is the standard night-view spot for the city and delivers consistently.

Himeji is thirty minutes by train and the castle there is the finest in Japan — white, intact, the approach through the outer gates building anticipation for the keep. The autumn harvest festival in the city, with the shrines being carried through the streets and lifted and bounced by the crowd, is one of the better festivals in the region. Mt. Shosha above Himeji, and the Engyoji temple complex at the top, are worth the separate trip.

The Akashi Kaikyo Bridge — the longest suspension bridge in the world — is visible from Kobe on a clear day and the observation deck on the bridge gives views across the strait to Awaji Island. The aquarium toilet at the observation point is exactly what it sounds like: a toilet cubicle surrounded on three sides by fish tanks. It is an experience.

The food in Kobe: raw whale meat, okonomiyaki, Nepalese food that was better than most Nepalese food anywhere. The sports: Vissel Kobe against Gamba Osaka and against Barcelona, Hanshin Tigers against Orix Buffaloes. The crowds at all three were excellent.

Fireworks over Harbourland — Kobe doing what Kobe does in the best possible way.

Himeji Castle — the finest castle in Japan, white and intact, thirty minutes from Kobe.

“Kobe was home for twelve months, and the city earned that designation without much effort.”

Kyoto — Temples in Every Season

Kyoto operates at a different pace from the rest of Japan and seems aware of it. The temples and shrines are too numerous to cover completely and the city rewards returning in different seasons — the cherry blossoms in spring, the deep red momiji of autumn, the quiet of winter — each season making the same places completely different.

Arashiyama holds the bamboo forest, which is as otherworldly in person as in photographs, and the monkey park above it where the macaques come to your hand for food. The golden pavilion of Kinkakuji is one of those things that photographs extensively and still looks better in person — the gold leaf on the water, the reflection, the whole composition. Fushimi Inari Taisha, with its thousands of torii gates winding up the mountain, is better experienced early in the morning before the crowds arrive, when the gates and the cedar forest around them produce a silence that the afternoon crowds disrupt.

Kiyomizudera on its wooden stage above the hillside is the iconic Kyoto image, and the walk down through the surrounding streets — the preserved machiya townhouses, the slope down to the Yasaka Pagoda visible in the distance — is the one to do at dusk. Toji Temple’s five-storey pagoda is the tallest wooden tower in Japan and dominates the surrounding neighbourhood.

Jojakkoji in autumn is the specific recommendation. The momiji here — the maple leaves at peak red — is something special, the colours more vivid and the crowds more manageable than the famous spots. The pagoda emerging from the red leaves with a rainbow visible above it is the kind of photograph that happens once. Haradani Gardens for the cherry blossoms offers a density of colour in spring that the larger parks don’t match.

The pagoda, the leaves, the rainbow — the kind of photograph that happens once.

Shiga — The Lake and the Ninja Village

Shiga wraps around Lake Biwa — the largest lake in Japan — and the prefecture is defined by it. Shirahige Shrine, sitting in the water of the lake with its torii gate rising from the surface, is one of the more picturesque shrines in the region and the kind of place where sitting and watching the light change for an hour feels like exactly the right use of time.

Hikone Castle is small and majestic — one of the few original castles remaining in Japan, its keep intact and the surrounding grounds pleasant. Yobai Falls requires a trek up the mountain and rewards the effort. The Shaun the Sheep Farm Garden near the lake is something nobody expects to find in rural Shiga, and finding it produces a particular kind of delight.

Koka Ninja Village is the highlight — the preserved buildings of an actual ninja training compound, an obstacle course, and the opportunity to throw ninja stars at wooden targets. The stars are for sale and several were purchased. Throwing them is harder than the films suggest.

Shirahige Shrine — the torii in the lake, picturesque and peaceful.

Shaun the Sheep Farm Garden — nobody expects to find this in rural Shiga, which is part of the appeal.

Osaka — The City That Eats Well

Osaka is the food capital of Japan, which is saying something. The city knows it and doesn’t let you forget — Dotonbori at night, the illuminated facades reflected in the canal, the smell of takoyaki from every other stall. Takoyaki here is the benchmark against which all other takoyaki should be measured.

Osaka Castle is one of the largest in Japan and the view from the top of the keep over the city parks below it is one of the better urban views in the region. The cherry blossoms in the castle park in spring are excellent. Katsuoji Temple is the hidden one — a hillside complex where darumas fill every surface, each one placed by someone making a wish, the accumulation of thousands of them across the buildings and paths creating something genuinely moving.

Universal Studios Japan delivers on the headliners. The Harry Potter area is immersive in the way that the best theme park areas are. Super Mario World is the visual spectacle of the park. The flying dinosaur ride is the one that requires actual courage and rewards it.

The Osaka Aquarium holds two whale sharks — two, in one tank — which is the largest display of its kind and produces exactly the reaction that watching two whale sharks swim in circles above you should.

Takoyaki — the benchmark here, measured against this every time afterward.

Nara — The Deer and the Buddha

Nara is the deer. Everything else is excellent — Todai-ji and the Great Buddha inside it are genuinely extraordinary, the building the largest wooden structure in the world and the bronze Buddha inside it the largest in Japan — but the deer that roam freely through Nara Park, bowing for the shika senbei crackers sold at every stall, are the thing that makes the city unlike anywhere else.

The deer are wild. They have learned that bowing produces crackers. They will bow at you, follow you, occasionally headbutt you when the crackers run out. They are everywhere and they are entirely comfortable with it.

The Yamayaki Matsuri — the festival in which the dry grass on the mountainside is set alight every January — is one of the more dramatic annual events in the region. The whole mountainside burning against a winter sky is a sight that earns the early morning visit. Mt. Yoshino in spring for the cherry blossoms is the other essential seasonal visit — the mountain covered in trees that bloom at slightly different times, the colours shifting across the hillside over several weeks.

Yamayaki Matsuri — the whole mountainside burning against a winter sky.

“The deer bow at you. They will follow you. They will headbutt you when the crackers run out.”

Wakayama — Blossoms, Falls, and the Coast

Wakayama is the quieter end of Kansai and rewards the visit. Kimiidera Temple in spring is one of the better cherry blossom sites in the region — the blossoms engulfing the shrine buildings, the steep steps up to the main hall lined on both sides with flowering trees, the whole approach framed in pink.

Nachi Falls is the tallest waterfall in Japan and the combination of the waterfall, the pagoda in front of it, and the cherry blossoms around the pagoda in spring is one of those compositions that earns the drive down the Kii Peninsula. Shirahama Beach closes the trip in the right register — the sun setting over the ocean, the white sand, the pace of a coastal town that knows what it has.

Shirahama Beach at sunset — the right way to end a Wakayama trip.

Kansai is the densest concentration of things worth seeing in Japan, and living in it for a year barely scratched the surface. Kobe as a base made everything accessible — Kyoto in every season, Osaka at any time, the quieter places that reward a day trip rather than a tourist itinerary. The shrines and temples are inexhaustible. The food is consistently excellent. The seasons make the same places completely different every few months.

A year here produced a list of things to go back to rather than a sense of having finished.

Kansai — the kind of region that keeps producing moments like this.
