Tohoku

The Remote North

Five days through Japan's least-visited region

June 2025 📍 Tohoku, Japan ✈️ 5 days

Miyagi — Cats, Foxes, and Floating Islands

Miyagi is where Tohoku starts making its case. Sendai is a lively city with good shopping and the Dai Kannon — a forty-metre white goddess statue on the hillside above the city — visible from a long way off. But the prefecture is best understood by what’s outside the city: Matsushima, Tashirojima, Akiu, and Zao, all within reach and all completely different from each other.

Matsushima is one of the three views traditionally considered the most beautiful in Japan — a bay scattered with hundreds of small pine-covered islands rising from the water, the iconic red bridge connecting the shore to the main island. It lives up to the designation.

Tashirojima is Cat Island, and that description is accurate rather than whimsical. The resident population of cats vastly outnumbers the human one, and they have the run of the entire island — following you on walks, sleeping on every available surface, entirely untroubled by visitors. The walk around the island is one of the better few hours in the whole trip.

Akiu Great Falls is secluded in a way that most waterfalls in Japan aren’t — the walk to it is short and the setting is quiet enough that dipping your feet in feels like something you’ve found rather than been directed to. Zao Fox Village is full of charming foxes and hard to fully enjoy given the feeling that they’d be better off in the wild. Go with managed expectations.

Matsushima — one of the three traditionally most beautiful views in Japan, and it earns it.

Akiu Great Falls — secluded, quiet, the kind of waterfall that feels found rather than visited.

Iwate — The Gorge and the Earthquake

Iwate carries the weight of the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake more visibly than anywhere else on the trip — the broken bridge and the road split in two are still there, left as reminders rather than cleared away, and standing in front of them is one of those moments where the scale of what happened becomes concrete in a way that statistics don’t manage.

Gembikei Gorge is the counterpoint: a narrow valley with a river running through green rock, utterly peaceful, and home to one of the more memorable food delivery systems in Japan — a small shop on one side of the gorge sends dango across on a rope pulley to visitors on the other side. The Akkoku-no-Iwaya Bishamondō shrine is built directly into the mountainside in the way that Tohoku shrines sometimes are, the rock face forming the back wall, the whole thing feeling like it grew out of the mountain rather than was placed on it.

Gembikei Gorge — narrow, green, and entirely peaceful.

The dango pulley — sent across the gorge on a rope by the shopkeeper on the other side.

Akita — Hot Springs and Near Misses

Oyasukyo is a gorge walk where the valley walls leak steam from the hot springs running through the rock — the path goes alongside the river at the base, the steam rising on both sides in the cold air, the whole thing feeling like walking through somewhere geologically active in real time. It is relaxing in the way that things are when they’re slightly otherworldly.

Mount Kurikoma is a longer commitment — the walk up to the hot spring shrine is beautiful, the views from the top are splendid, and at some point on the way there was a snake we nearly stoood on. The views were worth it regardless.

Mount Kurikoma — the hot spring shrine at the top, the snake somewhere on the way up.

Yamagata — A Thousand Steps and a Hot Spring Village

Yamadera is a temple complex built into the cliffs of a mountain above the Yamagata valley, and the thousand steps to reach it are the point rather than the obstacle. The views from the top — the valley below, the mountains beyond, the rooftops of the temple buildings emerging from the rock — are the kind that make the climb feel like a formality. It is one of the more rewarding temple visits in Japan.

Ginzan Onsen is a different experience entirely. The village sits in a narrow valley surrounded by mountains, a small river running through the centre, the ryokan buildings lining both banks — traditional wooden architecture, lanterns in the evening, hot spring steam rising from the water. It is beautiful in a way that photographs come close to but don’t quite capture, and the whole place feels like it was designed to exist exactly where it does.

“Beautiful in a way that photographs come close to but don’t quite capture.”

Fukushima — The Skyline Drive

The Bandai Azuma Skyline is a mountain road that runs through volcanic terrain above the cloud line, the landscape shifting between forest and bare rock and crater lakes as the road climbs. It is one of those drives where the scenery keeps demanding attention and the conditions — mist, sulphurous air, the occasional appearance of monkeys on the road — make it feel like somewhere the rules of normal geography don’t fully apply.

Lake Hibara sits in the valley below, and the light on it at sunrise and sunset is the kind that makes staying an extra morning feel like the obvious decision.

Bandai Azuma Skyline — the monkeys on the road, entirely unbothered by passing cars.

The Skyline — volcanic terrain, crater lakes, and the kind of scenery that keeps demanding attention.

“Somewhere the rules of normal geography don’t fully apply.”

Five days through a region that most visitors to Japan never reach, and every day different enough from the last that the variety starts to feel like the point. Tohoku is remote in the best sense — the distances between places are real, the crowds are not, and the things worth seeing are worth the effort of getting to them.

Tashirojima for the cats. Gembikei for the dango and the gorge. Ginzan Onsen for the evening. Yamadera for the view from the top. The broken bridge in Iwate for the reminder.

Ginzan Onsen — the image Tohoku leaves you with.
